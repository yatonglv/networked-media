NOSE

A scent drifts by, and a memory is instantly triggered—without my consent.
The scent of a certain laundry detergent takes me back to a summer long ago;
the smell of damp earth transports me to a place whose name I have long forgotten.
The sense of smell knows no logic; it simply snatches me back to a specific moment without warning,
then slips away as if nothing had happened.
I hate that musty, stifling smell when the washing machine has not dried the clothes properly—piled half-dry in the laundry basket,
an awkward scent somewhere between “clean” and “time to throw it away.”
The moment I catch a whiff of it, I can not help but frown, as if my body has made the judgment before my brain:
this task is not finished.
When my nose is stuffy, the world suddenly becomes half as quiet.
Coffee is no longer coffee, just a cup of hot liquid;
the hallway leading to my home loses that familiar,
indescribable scent, and my home itself seems to fade a little into the background.
For some reason, I love the smell of gas stations—that slightly pungent mix of gasoline and metal.
Others might find it overpowering, but to me, it really comforting.
I also love woody scents, like the blend of sandalwood and old wood you find in temples—as a child,
adults often took me to temples to burn incense, pay respects, and walk around the incense burners.
Back then, I did not understand the meaning of those rituals;
I only remember that smoky scent—a mix of wood and incense ash—creeping into my nose, quiet and deep.
Everyone wanted to pray for peace of mind and for their wishes to come true.
As for me, a little kid at the time, all I wanted to do was tell the Bodhisattva,
“You have worked so hard.”
With so many people needing help to make their wishes come true,
I just hoped there would be a little less suffering in the world—and that you could have it a little easier.
